# Pinyin: The Bridge Between Sound and Writing
## From Matteo Ricci to Zhou Youguang

Chinese is written in square characters. In each character, form and sound are separated. The first time you encounter a square sign, even something as simple as 日, you do not yet know how it is pronounced. Even if you live in a Chinese-speaking environment, even if you speak Chinese, even if you have heard the phrase “today is my birthday” many times and have eaten many birthday cakes, if no one has taught you that character, you still do not recognize it, you do not know that it reads as rì, and you do not understand its meaning. Not knowing one figure after another, even if you speak Chinese, if someone hands you a book, you still cannot read a single character. This is what illiteracy looks like in a Chinese-speaking context.

Different writing systems create different paths into reading. An English-speaking child who learns the 26 letters and the basic rules of phonics can begin reading short texts; a Chinese-speaking child must first learn the characters: recognize a character, remember how it is written, remember how it is pronounced, and understand what it means. Today they learn 日, tomorrow 生; only when these are put together do they understand “birthday.” The process is long and tedious, yet without learning several hundred characters, it is difficult to read even a short story. Foreigners learning Chinese follow the same path. Only after accumulating roughly three thousand characters, and being able to read books, journals, and newspapers, can one say that one's Chinese has reached a certain level.

Yet the appearance of Chinese phonetic notation offered Chinese learners another way to enter the language.

## I. The History of Pinyin: From Matteo Ricci to Zhou Youguang

In 1605, the Italian missionary Matteo Ricci made the first attempt to annotate Chinese characters with Latin letters.

This Jesuit wanted to bring Christianity to the people of China. He soon realized that only the educated elite had any real access to the possibility of missionary work. He understood that if he could not read Chinese characters, he would be unable to converse with the scholar-official class; if he could not understand them, he would be unable to enter the intellectual world of China. Thus he began his strategy of “entering through Confucianism”: he dressed in Confucian clothing, grew a long beard, studied the Four Books and the Five Classics, practiced calligraphy, and wrote Chinese texts. He even adopted the manners of Chinese literati to appear, as much as possible, like a “Confucian scholar from the West.”

In order to help more Europeans learn Chinese, he tried to break down the characters and use Latin letters to mark their pronunciation. He recorded the sounds of Chinese as he heard them in Latin letters. He wrote 天 as tien, 人 as gjin, 书 as xu, and 中国 as Tchong Kuo. These spellings were based on the Mandarin pronunciations he heard at the time, and they are not the same as modern Chinese pinyin, but they are a real record of how European ears tried to understand Chinese sounds.

If Matteo Ricci was the person who first tried to break Chinese characters apart by ear, then Zhou Youguang and other Chinese language scholars were the ones who turned this attempt into a systematic and unified method. More than three hundred years later, in 1955, in order to standardize Chinese pronunciation more effectively, Zhou Youguang led the development of the Chinese phonetic alphabet, or pinyin scheme. Zhou Youguang originally trained as an economist and later became a linguist; he spent many years working on language reform and modernizing written Chinese in the Chinese Committee for Language Reform. Under his leadership, a long-accumulated system of pronunciation was organized into a scientific, unified, and widely applicable scheme. This scheme began to be promoted in 1958 and became a foundational tool in Chinese children’s literacy education, as well as the first bridge for foreigners learning Chinese.

Pinyin is made up of three parts:

- **Initials** (consonants): b, p, m, f... form the starting point of the syllable
- **Finals** (vowels): a, o, e, ai, ang... form the main body of the syllable
- **Tones**: level, rising, falling-rising, and falling tones assign different meanings to the same syllable

For example, the syllable ma can produce completely different words depending on the tone:

mā (mother), má (hemp), mǎ (horse), mà (scold).

With a small number of symbols, pinyin records a highly rich system of Chinese sounds.

## II. The Function of Pinyin: Helping Children Enter the World of Reading Earlier

The simplest and most moving function of pinyin is that it helps us enter stories.

A child who has just learned pinyin can look at an annotated version of The Little Horse Crossing the River and sound out xiǎo mǎ guò hé by themselves, then read on: a little horse reaches a river, asks an old ox, asks a squirrel, and finally crosses it by himself. The reading is slow, but they can sound out one syllable at a time, one sound at a time. Then the plot unfolds, the story appears, and the door to reading gradually opens.

However, pinyin does not eliminate the difficulty of literacy. Chinese characters still have to be memorized one by one; their forms still have to be encountered repeatedly in reading, confirmed, and reinforced. But it lowers the threshold to reading: before learners have mastered a sufficient number of characters, they can still hear the sounds of Chinese, feel its rhythm, and begin to enter the language.

Reading, in turn, becomes a tool for learning characters. Children repeatedly encounter 日, 生, 河, and 马 in stories. The forms of the characters are absorbed naturally in context; the surrounding sentence helps them infer meaning by guessing. The first time is a guess, the second time is a confirmation, and the third time is a memory. 

Pinyin allows children to enter stories first; then, through repeated reading, the stories help children gradually come to know Chinese characters; the accumulation of characters lets them read longer stories; and longer stories bring with them more characters. This is a cyclical system that moves from sound to writing, and from phonetic reading to literacy.

Different writing systems create different ways into reading. The natural phonics system of English allows children to begin reading short texts after learning the 26 letters and the basic rules of sound-symbol correspondence. German has more regular pronunciation rules, and the correspondence between letters and sounds is stronger, so after learning the letters, children can read Haus, Wasser, Kind, and Tag with relative ease. Pinyin adds a similar path for Chinese: first entering reading through sound, and then moving from reading toward literacy.

## III. Fanqie, Zhuyin, and Pinyin

Modern Chinese pinyin is not the first time Chinese people tried to connect sound and writing. In ancient phonetic studies, “fanqie” worked like a pair of characters used to explain how another character should be pronounced: one character provided the initial sound, and another provided the final and tonal pattern. For example, 牵 can be annotated as 苦坚切: taking the initial k from 苦 and the final and tonal value from 坚, which gives qiān. It does not write sound in letters, but creates a bridge between one character and another by establishing a path of pronunciation. Its goal is the same as modern pinyin: to make “how it should be pronounced” visible, teachable, and transmissible.

Taiwan’s Zhuyin symbols are another modern educational system. They use a set of native symbols, similar in form to Chinese characters, to annotate Chinese writing. For example, the word 妈妈 is written as māma in mainland China, while in Taiwan it is usually written as ㄇㄚ ㄇㄚ. The same syllable structure is being expressed, but in different notation systems. Mainland pinyin emphasizes the correspondence between letters and pronunciation; Taiwan’s Zhuyin places more emphasis on the structure of syllables in teaching. The former is more suited to standardized transmission and foreign-language learning, while the latter is better suited to literacy and classroom instruction in a Chinese-speaking environment.

In other words, fanqie, pinyin, and Zhuyin are all attempts, in different historical periods, to solve the same problem: how to make spoken sound visible, teachable, and memorable.

## IV. Pinyin: The Bridge Between Sound and Writing

Pinyin turns the sound system of Chinese into something visible, readable, and operable. It is not a replacement for Chinese characters, but an auditory entry point into them.

From a linguistic perspective, pinyin has three main values:

- **Lowering the threshold of reading**: allowing children to enter reading before they have mastered many characters
- **Strengthening phonological awareness**: helping learners understand the structure of initials, finals, and tones in Chinese
- **Improving literacy efficiency**: allowing reading to reinforce memory of character forms

Pinyin allows learners to hear Chinese, read Chinese, and engage with Chinese before they have mastered a large number of characters. It adds a path from sound to meaning, complementing the path from form to meaning. It makes reading easier, helps stories enter more smoothly, and opens the language to learners in a more direct and accessible way.

Even after learning pinyin, we still need to memorize Chinese characters, because books, newspapers, and journals are not written with phonetic annotations, and classical texts are even less likely to include them. Pinyin is simply an aid on the path toward Chinese characters, stories, and language itself.
