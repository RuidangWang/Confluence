# **Neutrality Is Not a Moral Principle — It’s a Survival Strategy**

In late September 2026, Switzerland held a nationwide referendum. A proposal launched by the Swiss People’s Party to enshrine “strict and rigid neutrality” in the constitution was rejected by a large majority: more than 70% of voters opposed it, and none of Switzerland’s 26 cantons supported it.

Many people ask: what exactly is neutrality? In the popular imagination, neutrality means treating all warring parties equally after war breaks out, taking neither side, and refusing to participate in any restrictive measures directed at one of them. The People’s Party proposal seized on this plain understanding. It sought to lock strict neutrality into the constitution: forbidding entry into military alliances, prohibiting cooperation with defense blocs unless Switzerland itself suffers a direct military attack, and in principle banning unilateral sanctions against belligerents, allowing only sanctions authorized by the UN Security Council. The first clause targeted Switzerland’s recent peace partnership with NATO, limited joint military exercises, and equipment cooperation; the second targeted the country’s actions after the Russia–Ukraine war in 2022. Supporters argued that following the EU’s sanctions against Russia had broken Switzerland’s long-standing neutral tradition, damaged its identity as a mediator, and sacrificed business opportunities in the Russian market.

But the federal government, the overwhelming majority of mainstream parties, and the majority of voters who cast “no” votes saw the other side. They did not deny the costs of the sanctions: trade with Russia collapsed, Switzerland’s diplomatic assets as a mediator in the Russia–Ukraine conflict were sharply devalued, foreign investors lost confidence in the idea that “Swiss assets are absolutely safe,” and some capital fled abroad. But these were the unavoidable trade-offs of realism: sacrificing a smaller pie to preserve the larger one.

Switzerland is not an EU member state, yet it is completely surrounded by EU countries, has no coastline, and its economy is highly dependent on continental Europe. Pharmaceuticals, precision machinery, and watches—these pillar industries—depend most heavily on the EU as their largest export market. If the EU imposed collective sanctions on Russia while Switzerland refused to follow suit, the EU would have ample capacity to raise barriers through bilateral trade agreements, customs checks, and restrictions on cross-border movement, causing a major blow to Swiss trade. In 2020, when the pandemic caused a shortage of masks, a batch of Swiss masks was blocked at a German airport, illustrating the point. In the most extreme case, the appearance of cooperation and goodwill could be broken. An even more fatal constraint comes from the U.S.-dominated dollar financial system. The United States has secondary sanction power: even if Switzerland is a neutral country, if its banks provide financial services to sanctioned entities, Washington can directly restrict Swiss institutions from using dollars for settlement. For a country whose financial sector is centered on wealth management, this is a risk powerful enough to shake the foundations.

In simple terms, rejecting sanctions could preserve the short-term benefits of the Russian market, but in the long run it would expose Switzerland to the risk of EU trade barriers and U.S. financial pressure. When two harms are weighed against each other, the lesser evil prevails. In the end, the federal government chose to align with EU sanctions. The decision was not a dignified one, and it damaged the visible image of neutrality, but it protected the underlying foundations of the country’s economy and financial system.

Most voters who voted “no” were not endorsing these sanctions; they were rejecting the idea of locking one historical understanding of neutrality into the constitution forever. Today’s generation can assess the gains and losses of the Russia–Ukraine crisis, but no one can predict what future geopolitical conflicts will look like decades from now. If a rule such as “no sanctions without UN authorization” were written into the constitution, future generations facing entirely new crises would immediately lose diplomatic room for maneuver. Perhaps decades from now, the UN’s prestige may have fallen sharply. Today’s voters cannot strip future generations of their decision-making power. Neutrality cannot become a shackle that binds the hands and feet of those who come after us.

Switzerland’s tradition of neutrality did not begin as idealism at all; it was produced by the logic of great-power rivalry. In the Middle Ages, the Swiss region was not a peaceful land. Swiss mercenaries were prized elite troops hired by the major monarchs of Europe and were constantly drawn into continental struggles. One day they were hired by the French king to fight the Habsburgs; the next day they were hired by the Habsburg emperor to oppose the French. In the War of the Spanish Succession, at the Battle of Malplaquet in 1709, Swiss soldiers fought on both sides—on the French side and on the side of the anti-French coalition. Some 8,000 Swiss men killed one another. The turning point came at the Battle of Marignano in 1515, when Swiss mercenaries were defeated. This mountain confederation finally realized that continued involvement in great-power wars would only consume it further. The idea of staying out of great-power conflict took root.

With the Treaty of Westphalia in 1648, Switzerland gained independence. At the Congress of Vienna in 1815, the European powers formally recognized Swiss permanent neutrality. Many people think this was a sign of the great powers’ benevolence, recognizing that Switzerland loved peace. In fact, the opposite was true. The Alpine passes were a strategic corridor linking southern and central Europe. France, Prussia, Austria, and the other powers agreed that if any one power were to monopolize Switzerland, it would upset the balance of power in Europe. Making this region a buffer zone that no one could absorb served the common interest of all the great powers. Permanent neutrality was, in essence, a transaction among the great powers: Switzerland received guarantees against being swallowed up, in exchange for promising never to join a great-power military alliance and never to provoke war on its own initiative.

Neutrality treaties only set the most basic floor: countries must not initiate war and must not join military alliances. They did not define absolute equality in economic and diplomatic terms. This left room for later “flexible neutrality,” and flexibility was fully tested in the two world wars.

During World War I, Swiss society was naturally divided along linguistic lines, with large German-speaking and French-speaking populations. If it had chosen a side, the federation would likely have split apart. Switzerland therefore strictly adhered to the lower limit of non-participation, while sheltering refugees and maintaining trade with both sides. World War II was the most controversial period, and it became the starting point for later doubts about Swiss neutrality. Nazi Germany occupied most of Europe’s surrounding territories, and Switzerland was surrounded by Axis power on all sides. Switzerland mobilized the entire population, built defensive fortifications using mountain strongholds, and displayed the posture that attacking Switzerland would be extremely costly in order to deter the Germans. At the same time, it continued trading with Nazi Germany, importing coal and exporting industrial goods. Swiss banks also received assets from belligerents, including Nazi looted gold.

Many later critics argued that this was not neutrality at all, but trading with fascism. From the perspective of Switzerland at the time, it was a survival choice: if it had completely cut off economic ties with Germany, a resource-poor inland country would have struggled to sustain itself. Switzerland retained the core bottom line—the army did not fight. Above that line, economic exchange served national survival. It was also during this period that Swiss financial operations accumulated gold reserves and laid the foundation for the postwar rise of its financial sector. This chapter of history remains controversial to this day, and Swiss society continues to confront it.

After the Cold War, the boundaries of neutrality continued to shift. Switzerland did not join NATO, but it joined NATO’s Partnership for Peace and engaged in limited joint military exercises and equipment cooperation. In 2002, it passed a referendum to join the United Nations, ending its long period of staying outside the organization. These changes continued to widen the boundaries of flexible neutrality.

From this, we can see a core fact: the grand concept of neutrality is itself a human-made narrative, not a timeless, universally valid standard. Different eras and different political factions can all invoke this noble-sounding word to package their own interests. 

The People’s Party talks about neutrality in terms of preserving trade with Russia and protecting the Swiss banking industry’s reputation for “its assets being immune from geopolitical interference.” The federal government talks about neutrality in terms of preserving the EU market and protecting financial-system security. Both sides invoke “neutrality,” and both borrow its moral narrative, but neither is willing to place interest-based trade-offs plainly on the table. If the government simply said, “We are imposing sanctions on Russia because we fear U.S. secondary sanctions will destroy our banking industry and because we fear the EU will erect trade barriers,” the public would find it hard to accept. It sounds much more palatable to say, “We are defending the international order and practicing responsible neutrality.” The People’s Party would not openly say, “We want to protect our business with Russia,” but instead promotes “defending traditional neutrality and restoring Switzerland’s role as a peaceful mediator.”

Of course, this narrative is not a completely false cloak; it has real force. When the gap between policy and public expectations of neutrality becomes too large, the narrative begins to be overdrawn. After the 2022 sanctions, Switzerland’s credibility as an international mediator suffered, and confidence among global wealthy individuals in the safety of Swiss bank assets declined. That is the cost. This is also the root of the ongoing debate in Swiss society and the referendum that was initiated.

Looking at today’s international landscape, globalization and secondary sanctions are shrinking the buffer space available to small states. In the past, Switzerland could trade with both major powers, and belligerents found it difficult to punish a neutral country’s economic activity. Now, the U.S. dollar settlement system and the EU’s unified sanctions framework give great powers powerful financial weapons. Neutrality for small states no longer means that they can remain economically untouched by great-power conflict. The buffering value of neutrality is changing.

This referendum offers a warning to anyone observing international politics: do not casually treat grand concepts in international politics as eternal truths. Neutrality, sovereignty, and international morality are all tools created by human beings; their value depends on whether they can serve national survival and interests. Tools can be reinterpreted as circumstances change, but they should not be permanently nailed down in a single, rigid way.

Switzerland’s centuries of experience prove that a small country without vast territory or a large population, living in Europe surrounded by great powers, cannot rigidly cling to a single dogma. Flexible pragmatism is the core principle—holding the most fundamental security line while retaining the flexibility to adjust policy, weigh costs and benefits in the face of crisis, and make difficult trade-offs. That is the survival strategy Switzerland has continually iterated through the shifting waves of great-power rivalry. It may wear a respectable narrative shell, but underneath it is mostly a practical balancing of interests.
