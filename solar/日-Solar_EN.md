## The Same Sun, Two Starting Points of Civilization

Over three thousand years ago, a boy in the Yellow River valley pointed at the sky and asked his father, “What is this?” His father picked up a stone knife and carved a circle with a dot in the middle onto an oracle bone—“日.” It was a shape, a symbol, a form that could be seen, touched, and carved into objects.

In the same era, a boy in Mesopotamia also pointed at the sun and asked his mother, “What is that?” His mother did not carve a shape. Instead, she made a sound: “séhiwəl.” The boy repeated it, distinguishing the syllables and listening for the stress, until his mother nodded. It was a sound, a rhythm, a syllable that could only be heard, remembered, and passed down.

The same upward gaze, the same curiosity—but the ways civilizations expressed them parted company.

## **China: A Civilization Drawn**

The Chinese chose the path of the graphic form. They carved “日” onto oracle bones, cast it in bronze, and wrote it on bamboo slips. As the ages changed, oracle-bone script became bronze script, bronze script became small seal script, and small seal script became clerical script. The “日” of clerical script is exactly the same as the “日” we see today.

The graphic form stabilized, but its meaning kept expanding.

The sun rises and sets every day; people work at sunrise and rest at sunset. Thus “日” became time: one day, a day, yesterday, today, tomorrow. Further still, “日子” appeared—no longer mere timekeeping, but a further extension of meaning: the accumulation of day after day that makes up a life. “What day is it today?” “Live a good life.” The Chinese bound time and life together, turning the rhythm of the sun into the rhythm of life.

The Chinese continued along the path of the graphic form. They used “日、月、金、木、水、火、土” to draw the myriad things of heaven and earth; they used pictographs, simple indicatives, compound ideographs, and phono-semantic compounds to keep shaping symbols and creating characters; they used glyphs to carry meaning, and meaning and its extensions to record the world and experience.

Writing moved from oracle bones to bronze, from bronze to bamboo slips, from bamboo slips to silk, and then to paper. The appearance of paper, together with the maturity of the brush, let Chinese civilization flourish like a fish in water. Important matters were written down—black on white, clear and plain. Knowledge, insights, experience, and poetry were passed down generation after generation. Scholars had to read the works of their predecessors, studying until their hair turned white; cultured people, when speaking or writing, had to cite the classics. Writing was not merely a recording tool; it was the skeleton of civilization.

More importantly, characters directly defined words, tense, and grammar. In “黄河之水天上来” (“The waters of the Yellow River come from heaven”), the single character “来” makes the direction clear—coming from afar, drawing near. In “大江东去” (“The great river flows east”), the single character “去” makes the movement unmistakable. In “李白乘舟将欲行” (“Li Bai is about to set off in a boat”), the single character “将” already places the future tense there. This weak grammar leaves Western scholars, accustomed to tense changes and rigorous structure, at a loss as to where to begin.

What troubles Western scholars most is “他、她、它”—he, she, it—which also rests on the pictorial nature of Chinese characters. A reader sees at a glance whether it is male, female, or an object; no word-ending change is needed, no case change is needed. The graphic form itself states the meaning clearly. But a listener is utterly confused, unable to tell whether the speaker means a man, a woman, or their cat.

Chinese writing is more a “visual civilization.” It organizes the world with graphic forms and organizes thought with writing.

## **The West: A Civilization Heard**

The people of Mesopotamia chose another path—the path of sound. What they remembered was the sound “séhiwəl,” not its shape. The sound was passed down generation after generation, becoming Sonne, becoming Sun, becoming solar. The transmission of sound is direct, from mother to child—stable and effective, uninterrupted even by war. Writing, by contrast, requires writing and safekeeping, which is why many Chinese classics were lost or damaged in times of war. The Homeric epics, meanwhile, were sung for more than four hundred years before they were recorded as books.

Sound is linear: one syllable after another, gone as soon as it is heard. To let listeners understand meaning before the sound disappears, Western languages developed a rigorous grammatical system:

- subject-verb-object order, so that you know at a single hearing who did what;
- case changes, such as German der, die, das, so that you know upon hearing whether the speaker means a man, a woman, or an object;
- tense changes, so that you know upon hearing whether it is past, present, or future;
- word-ending changes, so that you know upon hearing the relationships of subject, object, and possessive.

Many German sentence patterns place the verb at the end. You must listen all the way to the final syllable before you can understand the meaning of the whole sentence. This is a language designed for the ear, keeping the listener’s attention until the speaker’s words end.

Sound drifts through history, syllables deform in the wind, but grammar, etymology, and word formation stand firm, like an invisible riverbed. This is more an “auditory civilization.” It organizes the world with sound and organizes thought with grammar.

## **Myths Mirroring Each Other: Helios and the Golden Crow**

Though the methods of description differ, the sensory experience the sun gives us is the same. It shines over the earth, bringing us light and warmth. It rises in the east and sets in the west, steady and reliable; the sun brings us rhythm and order. This is something shared by Eastern and Western civilizations. And so both civilizations wove their own stories for the sun.

In Greek myth, Helios drives a golden sun chariot pulled by four fiery steeds. At dawn he rises from the eastern ocean, radiant, crossing the sky; at dusk he falls into the western sea. At night he rides a great golden boat, returning from the sea beneath the earth to the east, and the next day he rises again. He is not only a symbol of light; he sees all, perceives all, witnesses all.

In China’s oldest myths, the sun is a shining bird, a three-legged divine bird named the Golden Crow. It has three legs (symbolizing “three,” the yang number in Eastern culture). Its body is like a crow’s, but its feet tread flame. Every day it takes off from the Fusang tree, crosses the sky, and at dusk falls into the Guixu beneath the Ruo tree.

Interestingly, both East and West imagined the sun as “a living thing walking across the sky.” The West had it ride a chariot; China had it spread its wings. Different images, yet both try to answer the same question: Why can the sun rise from the east and set in the west every day? From the sun’s warmth and light, how do we understand the order of life and the light of morality?

A chariot, a bird—two civilizations’ imaginations brush past each other in the sky.

## **The Same Sun, Different Expressions**

Three thousand years ago, two boys asked the same question: “What is that?”

One first received a shape; one first received a sound.  
One leaned more toward organizing the world with images; one leaned more toward organizing thought with sound.  
One drew the sun into time and life; one drew the sun toward divine will and grammar.  
One let meaning settle more in the glyph; one let meaning flow more in the syllable.

Today, we learn each other’s languages and read each other’s stories,  
and in doing so, we are answering that ancient question again:  
that shining thing—how should we call it, write it, understand it?

The sun lights the same sky,  
and we once walked different roads in its light,  
yet again and again, in each other’s words and sounds,  
we see the same sun anew.
