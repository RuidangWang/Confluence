# **日-Solar** -- Writing, Sound, Myth

Wang Mianmian
2026.09.19

Every day the sun rises, quietly shaping the way we understand the world. The Chinese put it into writing; the Western world preserved it in sound. One character, one sound, one solar god, one golden bird. Today, let us begin with the sun and ask how two civilizations tell the story of the same world.

## **The same sun, two civilizations, two beginnings**

Three thousand years ago, in the Yellow River basin, a boy pointed at the sky and asked his father, “What is that?” His father lifted a stone knife and carved a circle with a dot in the middle on a turtle shell—“ri” (sun). It was a shape, a symbol, something that could be seen, touched, and inscribed on objects.

At the same time, in the river valleys of the ancient Near East, a boy also pointed at the sun and asked his mother, “What is that?” His mother did not draw a shape. She uttered a sound: “séhiwəl.” The boy followed her, distinguishing syllables and listening for stress until she nodded. It was a sound, a rhythm, a syllable that could be heard, remembered, and recited.

The same upward gaze, the same curiosity—but the ways civilizations expressed them diverged.

## **China: a civilization drawn in images**

The Chinese chose the path of the graphic form. They carved the character “ri” onto oracle bones, cast it in bronze, and wrote it on bamboo slips. Over time, oracle-bone script became bronze script, bronze script became seal script, and seal script became clerical script. The “ri” in clerical script is almost the same as the character we see today.

The form stabilized, but its meaning kept expanding.

Every day the sun rises and sets; people go out at sunrise and rest at sunset. So “ri” became time: one day, a day, yesterday, today, tomorrow. Further still came “ri zi” (days, life)—no longer simply a measure of time, but a broader extension of meaning: the accumulation of days that forms a life. “What day is it today?” “Live a good life.” The Chinese tied time to life itself, turning the rhythm of the sun into the rhythm of existence.

The Chinese continued along the path of graphic form. They used “sun, moon, metal, wood, water, fire, earth” to map the world; using pictographs, indicators, associative compounds, and semantic-phonetic characters, they kept shaping symbols and creating writing. Characters carried meaning; meaning extended onward to record the world and experience.

Writing moved from oracle bones to bronze, from bronze to bamboo slips, then to silk, and finally to paper. The spread of paper, together with the maturity of the brush, lowered the cost of writing and transmission. Important matters could be recorded; knowledge, experience, and poetry could be passed down from generation to generation. Documents and classics grew ever more important. Scholars had to read the works of their predecessors; literati, in speech and writing, relied on the authority of the classics and allusions. Writing was not merely a tool of record; it was the very skeleton of civilization.

Chinese attention to meaning shaped its grammar: it does not rely primarily on inflectional changes to mark grammatical function, but more on meaning, context, and word order. “The water of the Yellow River comes from heaven” — the word “comes” makes the direction clear, from afar toward us; “The great river flows east” — the word “flows” makes the movement unmistakable; “Li Bai was about to set sail” — the word “about to” already carries the sense of futurity. For speakers of languages shaped by tense changes and grammatical case, this kind of expression often has to be understood from meaning and context rather than from inflectional endings.

Chinese writing is more a “visual civilization.” It organizes the world through form and organizes thought through writing.

## **The West: a civilization heard in sound**

The people of the Near East chose another path—the path of sound. What they remembered was the sound “séhiwəl,” not its shape. That sound was passed down from mother to child, becoming Sonne, then Sun, then solar. The transmission of sound was direct and stable, effective even during upheaval. Writing, in contrast, required inscription and preservation, which is why many Chinese classics were lost or damaged in times of war. The Homeric epics, meanwhile, were sung for more than four hundred years before being written down.

Sound is linear: one syllable follows another, and once heard, it is gone. To make meaning intelligible before the sound disappears, Western languages developed a grammar more dependent on structure and inflection. In English, the sentence “The father gives the book to his son” makes the relationships clear through word order and prepositions: who gives, what is given, and to whom. Subject-verb-object order and fixed verb position require the listener to hear the full sentence before understanding the relation. Unlike Chinese, which depends more on lexical meaning and context, Western languages rely more on structure, pressing meaning into syntax.

Sound drifts through history; syllables deform in the wind. But grammar, etymology, and word formation remain steady, like an unseen riverbed. This is more an “auditory civilization.” It organizes the world through sound and thought through grammar.

## **Myths mirrored across the sky: the solar god and the solar bird**

Though the methods differ, the sensory experience of the sun is the same. It fills the earth with light and warmth. It rises in the east, sets in the west, steady and reliable; the sun brings rhythm and order. This is something both civilizations share. And so both wove their own stories about the sun.

In Greek myth, Helios drives a golden solar chariot pulled by four flaming horses. At dawn he rises from the eastern sea, radiant and blazing, crossing the sky; at dusk he sinks into the western ocean. At night he rides a vast golden boat beneath the earth, returning to the east, and the next day he rises again. He is not only the symbol of light; he sees all things, knows all things, and witnesses all.

In the oldest Chinese myths, the sun is a shining bird, a three-legged divine bird called the Golden Crow. It has three legs (symbolizing “three,” the yang number in Eastern culture), a body like a raven’s, yet it stands on flame. Every day it takes off from the Fusang tree, crosses the sky, and at dusk descends into the underworld beneath the Ruomu tree.

What is interesting is that both East and West imagine the sun as a living thing moving across the sky. The West has it ride a chariot; China has it spread its wings. Different images, yet the same question: why does the sun rise in the east and set in the west every day? From the warmth and light of the sun, how do we understand the order of life and the morality of light?

A chariot, a bird—the imaginations of the two civilizations brush past each other in the sky.

## **The same sun, different expressions**

Three thousand years ago, two boys asked the same question: “What is that?”

One first received a shape; the other first received a sound.  
One leaned more toward organizing the world with images; the other toward organizing thought with sound.  
One drew the sun into time and daily life; the other carried it toward divine meaning and grammar.  
One let meaning settle into the form of the character; the other let it flow through the syllable.

Today, as we learn each other’s languages and read each other’s stories,  
we are still answering the same ancient question:  
how should we name that shining thing, write it down, and understand it?

The sun illuminates the same sky,  
and we once walked different roads in its light,  
yet again and again, in each other’s words and sounds,  
we see the same sun anew.
