# **日-Solar** -- Writing, Sound, Myth

Wang Mianmian  
2026.09.19

Every day the sun rises, quietly shaping the way we understand the world. The Chinese wrote it into characters; the Western world preserved it in sound. One character, one sound, one solar god, one golden bird. Today, let us begin with the sun and see how two civilizations tell the story of the same world.

## **The same sun, two civilizations, two beginnings**

More than three thousand years ago, a boy in the Yellow River basin pointed at the sky and asked his father, “What is that?” His father took up a stone knife and carved a circle with a dot in the middle on a turtle shell—“ri” (sun). It was a shape, a symbol, something visible, tangible, and capable of being engraved on objects.

At roughly the same time, a boy in the river valleys of the ancient Near East also pointed at the sun and asked his mother, “What is that?” His mother did not carve a shape. She uttered a sound: “séhiwəl.” The boy followed her, distinguishing syllables and listening for stress until she nodded. It was a sound, a rhythm, a syllable that could be heard, remembered, and recited.

The same upward gaze, the same wonder—but the ways civilizations expressed it diverged.

## **China: a civilization drawn in images**

The sun rises and sets every day; people work at sunrise and rest at sunset. So “ri” became time: one day, a day, yesterday, today, tomorrow. Even further, “ri zi” (days, life) emerged—no longer just a unit of measurement, but an extension of meaning: the accumulation of days becomes life itself. “What day is it today?” “Live a good life.” The Chinese tied time to life, turning the rhythm of the sun into the rhythm of existence.

Chinese civilization continued along the path of the graphic form. They used “sun, moon, metal, wood, water, fire, earth” to map the world; they shaped symbols through pictographs, indicative characters, associative compounds, and semantic-phonetic characters; they created writing; and they used the forms of characters to carry meaning, extending that meaning to record the world and experience.

Writing developed from oracle bones and bronze vessels to bamboo slips and silk, and eventually to paper. The maturity of paper and the brush lowered the cost of writing and transmission, allowing important matters to be preserved and knowledge, experience, and poetry to be passed down through the generations. Classics and records became increasingly important, and scholars increasingly relied on inherited authority and textual citation. Writing was not only a tool for recording; it became the skeleton of civilization.

The Chinese language’s emphasis on meaning shaped its grammar: rather than relying primarily on morphological changes to mark grammatical function, it depends more on meaning, context, and word order. “The Yellow River’s water comes from heaven,” “The great river flows east,” “Li Bai, in the boat, is about to set sail.” Each character and word makes direction, movement, and temporal sense clear. For speakers of languages that rely on tense and case inflection, such expression is often understood through meaning and context rather than through formal endings.

Chinese writing is more a “visual civilization.” It organizes the world through shapes and organizes thought through writing.

## **The West: a civilization heard in sound**

The people of the ancient Near East took another path—the path of sound. What they remembered was the sound “séhiwəl,” not its shape. That sound was passed down from mother to child, becoming Sonne, then Sun, then solar. The transmission of sound was direct and stable, effective even amid upheaval. Writing, by contrast, required inscription and preservation, which is why many Chinese classics were lost or damaged in times of war. The Homeric epics, meanwhile, were sung for more than four hundred years before being written down.

Sound is linear: one syllable follows another, and once heard, it is gone. To make meaning clear before the sound disappears, Western languages developed a grammar that depends more heavily on structure and inflection. In English, the sentence “The father gives the book to his son” makes the relations clear through word order and prepositions: who gives, what is given, and to whom. The subject-verb-object sequence and verb position require the listener to hear the entire sentence before understanding the relation. Unlike Chinese, which relies more on lexical meaning and context, Western languages rely more on structure and pack meaning into syntax.

Sound drifts through history; syllables change in the wind. But grammar, etymology, and word formation remain stable, like an unseen riverbed. This is more an “auditory civilization.” It organizes the world through sound and organizes thought through grammar.

## **Myths mirrored in the sky: the solar god and the solar bird**

Although the methods differ, the sensory experience of the sun is the same. It fills the earth with light and warmth. It rises in the east and sets in the west, steady and reliable; the sun brings rhythm and order. Both civilizations share this recognition. That is why both wove their own stories around the sun.

In Greek mythology, Helios drives a golden solar chariot pulled by four fiery horses. At dawn he rises from the eastern sea, radiant and blazing, crossing the sky; at dusk he sinks into the western ocean. At night he rides a vast golden boat beneath the earth, returning to the east, and the next day he rises again. He is not only the symbol of light; he sees all things, knows all things, and witnesses all.

In the oldest Chinese myths, the sun is a shining bird, a three-legged divine bird called the Golden Crow. It has three legs, a body like a raven’s, and it stands upon flame. Every day it takes flight from the Fusang tree, crosses the sky, and at dusk descends beneath the Ruomu tree in the underworld.

What is interesting is that both East and West imagine the sun as a living thing moving across the sky. The West has it ride a chariot; China has it spread its wings. Different images, but the same question: why does the sun rise in the east and set in the west every day? And from the sun’s warmth and light, how do we understand the order of life and the morality of illumination?

A chariot, a bird—the imaginations of two civilizations brush past each other in the sky.

## **The same sun, different expressions**

Three thousand years ago, two boys asked the same question: “What is that?”

One first received a shape; the other first received a sound.  
One leaned more toward organizing the world through images; the other toward organizing thought through sound.  
One drew the sun into time and daily life; the other carried it toward divine meaning and grammar.  
One allowed meaning to settle into the form of the character; the other let it flow through the syllable.

Today, as we learn one another’s languages and read one another’s stories,  
we are still answering that ancient question:  
How should we name that shining thing, write it down, and understand it?
